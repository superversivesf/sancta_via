Edith Stein (German: [ʃtaɪn]; religious name Teresa Benedicta of the Cross; 12 October 1891 – 9 August 1942) was a German philosopher who converted to Catholicism and became a Discalced Carmelite. She was murdered in the gas chamber at the Auschwitz II-Birkenau concentration camp on 9 August 1942, and is canonized as a martyr of the Catholic Church; she is also one of six patron saints of Europe.
Stein was born into an observant German Jewish family, but gave up prayer in her teenage years.
After World War I broke out, she trained as a nursing assistant  and worked in an Austro-Hungarian field hospital in 1915. After completing her doctoral thesis at the University of Freiburg in 1916, she obtained an assistantship to Edmund Husserl there.
From reading the life of the reformer of the Carmelites, Teresa of Ávila, Stein chose to convert to the Roman Catholic Church. She was baptised on 1 January 1922. At that point, she wanted to enter a Carmelite monastery as soon as possible, but this was only possible for newly converted with a special dispensation; furthermore she was dissuaded by her spiritual director, Josef Schwind, a canon of the Speyer Cathedral. He told her that her work in the world was too important. Stein then taught at a Catholic school of education in Speyer. As a result of the requirement of an "Aryan certificate" for civil servants promulgated by the Nazi government in April 1933 as part of its Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, she had to quit her teaching position after eight years of work.
Edith Stein was admitted as a postulant to the Discalced Carmelite monastery in Cologne on 25 November, on the first Vespers of the feast of Saint Teresa of Ávila, and received the religious habit as a novice in April 1934, taking the religious name Teresia Benedicta a Cruce (Teresia in remembrance of Teresa of Ávila, Benedicta in honour of Benedict of Nursia). She made her temporary vows on 21 April 1935, and her perpetual vows on 21 April 1938.
The same year, Teresa Benedicta a Cruce and her biological sister Rosa, by then also a convert and an extern (tertiary of the order, who would handle the community's needs outside the monastery), were sent to the Carmelite monastery in Echt, Netherlands, for their safety. In response to the pastoral letter from the Dutch bishops on 26 July 1942, in which they made the treatment of the Jews by the Nazis a central theme, all baptised Catholics of Jewish origin (according to police reports, 244 people) were arrested by the Gestapo on the following Sunday, 2 August 1942. They were sent to the Auschwitz concentration camp, and were murdered in the Birkenau gas chambers on 9 August 1942. Stein was beatified in 1987 and canonized in 1998 by Pope John Paul II.


== Early life ==

Edith Stein was born into a Jewish family on Yom Kippur, October 12, 1891, in Breslau, in the German Empire (now Wrocław, Poland),  She was the youngest of 11 children, seven of whom lived to adulthood. Her father Siegfried, died before her second birthday, leaving her mother, Auguste to run the family lumber business. Though Auguste was devout, Edith appears to have received minimal religious training in Judaism.
Stein entered the Viktoriaschule, a girls' school in Breslau, at age six. In 1906, she completed her secondary education at fourteen, and took a year-long break to live with her non-religious older sister and brother-in-law in Hamburg, where she consciously gave up prayer. Upon returning to Breslau, she entered the Gymnasium to prepare for university, graduating in March 1911; with written exams scores high enough to exempt her from oral exams.


== Academic career ==


=== Breslau, 1911–1913 ===

In April 1911, Stein entered the Schlesische Friedrich-Wilhelms-Universität (Silesian Friedrich Wilhelm University) in Breslau, to study psychology, history, and German studies.  During her first semester, she attended Richard Hönigswald's natural philosophy course. She also studied psychology with William Stern, her first choice for dissertation advisor, who introduced her to his "Pedagogical Group" exploring teacher education, an association she considered the highlight of her time at the university.
During a 1911 psychology seminar on thought associated with the Würzburg, Stein encountered many references to the philosopher Edmund Husserl's Logical Investigations. She was also inspired to learn that a woman, Hedwig Conrad-Martius, had won a prestigious essay prize from the University of Göttingen philosophy faculty. Soon after reading a cousin's letter inviting her to study at Göttingen, Stein decided to spend her upcoming summer there. During Christmas break, she familiarized herself with phenomenology
by reading the second volume of Logical Investigations. Before departing for Göttingen, she asked Stern to serve as her dissertation advisor; he proposed she study the development of children's thoughts; en route, she stopped at the Institute of Applied Psychology near Potsdam to meet with Otto Lipmann, one of Stern's collaborators.


=== Göttingen, 1913–1915 ===


==== Summer 1913 ====

Arriving at the University of Göttingen in April 1913 for the summer semester, the 21-year-old Stein introduced herself to Adolf Reinach, a privatdozent in philosophy. Reinach helped Stein secure a spot in Husserl's summer seminar, which was influenced by his newly published work, Ideas I. In this work, Husserl aimed to build a critique of naturalism and objectivity that would replace psychology's claim as an objective science with a more rigorous transcendental science of subjectivity.  
Stein also took a history seminar with Max Lehmann, looking at the Constitution of the German Empire, who recommended her paper  analyzing the party programs of the 1849 Frankfurt Constitution  as a state board examination thesis. She joined the Philosophical Society, a group of Husserl's students, attending off-campus lectures by Max Scheler, who was banned from the society due to personal indiscretions. Scheler had just published a phenomenological study on empathy. Despite her reservations about his behavior, his lectures opened her to Catholic thought and directly inspired her own interest in  empathy. By the end of the summer, she had decided to write her dissertation under Husserl on empathy, officially informing Stern she was leaving psychology.


==== October 1913–January 1915 ====
Stein returned to Göttingen for the 1913–1914 winter semester after a brief stay in Breslau. She grew closer to Reinach, who mentored her in the basics of phenomenology, and strengthened connections with fellow phenomenology students, including Hans Lipps, Fritz Kaufmann, and Roman Ingarden; she also met Jean Héring, who briefly came to take his state exams. While preparing for her own state exams,  Stein focused on her dissertation, contrasting her phenomenological view of empathy with the perspective of Theodor Lipps, the major theorist at that time. Reinach's constant support sustained her through periods of despair and suicidal ideation.
When Germany declared a state of war at the end of July 1914, classes were cancelled. Stein returned to Breslau to register for Red Cross nursing to support the German war effort. Before being called up, she returned to Göttingen and completed her state examinations with highest honors in January 1915. During this time she also served as the vocational counselor for women students for the Frauenbildung-Frauenstudium-Verein [Society for Women's Education- Women's Studies].


=== Interlude: Red Cross service, April–September 1915 ===

Upon returning to Breslau after her state exams, Stein was called up to serve as a volunteer nurse in an Austro-Hungarian military reserve hospital in Mährisch Weißkirchen, arriving in early April 1915. She worked in the typhus ward, transferring to an operating room three months later. Furloughed after six months of service, she earned a Medal of Valor for her dedication and expected to return, but the hospital closed when the military front shifted east.
Her service exposed her to an ethnically and linguistically diverse environment. Though the nurses and aides were German, the hospital operated under Czech leadership and was mostly staffed with Czech and Polish doctors, treating patients from the various ethnolinguistic groups across the empire, as well as Russians, Turks, and Romanies. Stein relied on a nine-language manual to communicate with those she served.


=== Breslau, September 1915–July 1916 ===
Stein spent the remainder of 1915 writing her dissertation. During Christmas, she visited Adolf Reinach,  who had volunteered to serve in the German Army and was on leave with his wife Anna. She also met with Husserl to discuss her Red Cross service in his native Moravia. While teaching Latin, German, history, and geography at the Realgymnasium at the Victoriaschule in Breslau, she finished the manuscript during the 1916 Easter break and sent it to Husserl at his new post at the University of Freiburg.


=== Freiburg, 1916–1918 ===
In July 1916, Stein traveled to the University of Freiburg during the summer break to take her doctoral oral examination. Upon arriving, she discovered that Husserl had not yet read her dissertation, though he agreed to do so with prodding from his wife Malvine. Stein set the examination for August 3, so she could return to her teaching before the break was over. Meanwhile, she attended Husserl's summer seminar and met Martin Heidegger at a social event hosted by the Husserls. 
Before the oral examination, Husserl praised her dissertation, prompting Stein to propose being his literary assistant and collaborator, an offer Husserl accepted. After passing her exam, she was awarded a PhD, summa cum laude. The following year, she published three of the seven dissertation chapters   as On the Problem of Empathy, while the four unpublished chapters on community, aesthetics, and ethics, as well as the literature review, were subsequently lost. Returning to Breslau after her defense, Stein was officially released from her teaching duties at the end of September.

Stein returned to Freiburg in October 1916 as Husserl's private research assistant. Receiving a token salary, she relied on an allowance from her mother. She taught a beginning seminar in phenomenology, but her main responsibility was to organize his research manuscripts. Because much of the material consisted of unpublishable scraps written in Gabelsberger shorthand, she organized, edited, and clarified them, frequently consulting Husserl. Her major contributions included Ideas II, posthumously published in 1952, and On the Phenomenology of Time Consciousness, published in 1928 with Heidegger credited as the editor.
During this time, Stein learned that Reinach had died in November 1917, a loss that deeply moved her and marked a turning point in her life. Adolf and Anna Reinach had converted to Protestantism in 1916, and while helping Anna organize his literary legacy, Stein was struck by how Anna's faith helped her bear her grief. In February 1918, after reading Reinach's religio-philosophical notes on how the war brought him to faith, Stein began to identify herself as a Christian. In that same month, she resigned her position with Husserl, having expressed dissatisfaction with the work in her private letters.


=== Independent scholar: Breslau, Göttingen, Freiburg, 1918–1920 ===

After resigning her assistantship in 1918, Stein traveled frequently between Breslau, Göttingen, and Freiburg,  mainly living with her family in Breslau. She joined the German Democratic Party, actively engaging in youth organization, coalition building, and shaping the party's position on issues like women's suffrage.
In the months following Reinach's death, Stein prepared a commemorative edition of his writings, reaching out to former Göttingen students like Hedwig Conrad-Martius and Jean Héring.; cf., p. 69: "entfaltet die Idee, gemeinsam mit anderen Schülern Reinachs einen Gedenkband zu Ehren Reinachs zu erarbeiten.26 Da Conrad-Martius und Hering dem Plan skeptisch gegenüber stehen, entwickelt sich eine intensive Diskussion." "[Stein proposed the idea of collaborating with Reinach's other students to produce a commemorative volume of his works in his honor. As Conrad-Martius and Héring were skeptical of the plan, an intense discussion ensued.]"; see also Letters to Ingarden 2014, April 10, May 5, & June 2, 1918. Reinach's Collected Writings was published in 1921 with an introduction by Conrad-Martius. Stein also initiated a Festschrift for Husserl's sixtieth birthday in 1919, enlisting contributors, convincing Alexander Pfänder to serve as  editor, and committing to an essay on psychic causality. In late 1920, she helped Hans Lipps write the manuscript required for his habilitation, the Habilitationsschrift, which secured him a teaching position in Göttingen.
Completed in 1919, Stein's two manuscripts, Psychic Causality and The Individual and Community, were submitted as her Habilitationsschrift to teach at Freiburg and Göttingen. Her application was officially rejected because she was a woman.  After Stein protested to Berlin and cited mathematician Emmy Noether's successful habilitation, the minister of education issued a supportive note to universities. Nevertheless, her candidacy failed due to anti-Jewish prejudice, faculty wariness, academic politics, and Husserl's lack of support, as he doubted the appropriateness of academic careers for women.
Stein gave private lessons in phenomenology and worked on a third manuscript, An Investigation Concerning the State, in 1920. Drawing on her phenomenological training under Reinach and Lehmann, Stein attempted to address Germany's post-war political instability. The two treatises from her Habilitationsschrift were published in 1922; her treatise on the state appeared in 1925.


== Bergzabern and conversion to Catholicism ==


=== The Bergzabern Circle, 1920–1921 ===

While preparing Reinach's writings in 1920, Stein was invited to Bergzabern by Conrad-Martius, who had moved to a fruit farm with her husband Theodor after being denied a university position as a woman. Arriving in late summer for a few months, Stein met Conrad-Martius in person for the first time while both were experiencing an existential crisis that hindered their ability to do philosophy, leading the two to form a close relationship.
In summer 1921, Stein returned to Bergzabern for an extended stay. On her way, she stopped to visit Anna Reinach, who invited her to choose a book from the family library as a gift. She selected Teresa of Ávila's autobiography and took it with her to the farm. 
During these months, she reconnected with other former Göttingen students, including Alfred von Sybel, Lipps, Hering, and Alexandre Koyré, whom she had not met before,  forming a supportive network connected by Reinach's influence known as the Bergzabern Circle. She also engaged in intense conversations with Conrad-Martius, who was developing her theory of the person as body, soul, and spirit for her book, Metaphysical Conversations.


=== Conversion and baptism, 1921–1922 ===

Reading Teresa of Ávila's autobiography at the Conrads' home prompted Stein to convert to the Roman Catholic Church. During the rest of her stay, she attended Mass at the Parish Church of St. Martin and studied a catechism and the missal, frequently seeking the quiet of the evangelical Bergkirche to read the Bible on her own. Returning to Breslau, she began writing Freedom and Grace, a phenomenological treatment on religious commitment. 
Upon returning to Bergzabern in October, the thirty-year-old Stein requested baptism from Pastor Eugen Breitling, who baptised her at St. Martin's on January 1, 1922, where she took the baptismal name Theresia Hedwig. Conrad-Martius received special dispensation to serve as her godmother, loaning Stein her white wedding cloak for the ceremony. She received her first Holy communion the next day and was confirmed by Ludwig Sebastian, Bishop of Speyer, in his private chapel on February 2. Breitling introduced her to Joseph Schwind, Cathedral Canon and Custodian of Speyer, as her spiritual director.


== Teaching Career ==


=== Speyer, 1923–1931 ===

After her baptism, Stein expressed a desire to join a Discalced Carmelite monastery, but Schwind advised her to place her talents at the service of the larger Catholic world. Following a brief period of teaching at the Victoriaschule in Breslau, Schwind helped her secure a position at the Dominican Convent of St. Magdalena in Speyer. Beginning in spring 1923, she taught German and history at the girls' secondary school, as well as Latin and philosophy at the teachers' training college, which left her little time for original philosophical work over the next few years.
From 1923 to 1925, Stein translated the writings of John Henry Newman into German at the invitation of Dietrich von Hildebrand, a Göttingen phenomenology alumnus who had converted to Catholicism. Hildebrand connected her with Jesuit Erich Przywara, who was co-editing a German edition of Newman's works, leading to their translation of The Idea of a University, along with several letters and Newman's autobiography. Przywara visited Stein in Speyer in 1925, where both agreed on the urgent need for dialogue between traditional Catholic philosophy and contemporary phenomenology. 
Encouraged by Przywara, she requested a reduction of teaching hours to translate Thomas Aquinas's Quaestiones Disputatae de Veritate (Disputed Questions Concerning Truth), transposing Thomistic language into a phenomenological idiom. Over the following years, Przywara and Stein would explore how scholastic concepts, particularly the analogia entis (The analogy of being), could open a contemporary philosophical discussion of the relationship between God and human existence. These explorations would influence their later publications, including Przywara's Analogia Entis.
Stein remained closely connected with Bergzabern, visiting the Conrads' farm during school breaks. There, she and Conrad-Martius translated an essay by Koyré into German in 1923, and in 1925, members of the Bergzabern Circle met at the farm to review papers by Lipps and Ingarden. In 1928, the group gathered again to coordinate contributions for Husserl's 70th birthday Festschrift. When Stein later entered the Carmelite Monastery in Cologne, she informed the Conrads that she could no longer visit, but invited them to visit her.
Following Schwind's death in 1927, Stein turned to Raphael Walzer, the Archabbot of the Benedictine abbey in Beuron, at Przywara's recommendation. Between 1928 and 1933, she visited the abbey over twenty times, often on solemnities and feasts to participate in the liturgy with the monks. Like Schwind, Walzer counseled her to remain in the world rather than enter Carmel. Prompted by Przywara to study the role of women in society and the Church, she established herself as an international lecturer across Germany and Austria, presenting papers on women's vocation and educational principles, which she actively applied to her own teaching. During this period, she also contributed an essay, Husserl and Aquinas: A Comparison, to Husserl's 1929 Festschrift.


=== Second attempt at habilitation, 1931–1932 ===
Seeking to continue her work on Thomism and phenomenology,  she started a new habilitation during her visit to Freiburg for Husserl's 70th birthday. There, Emeritus Professor of History Heinrich Finke advised her not bury her talents, which led to a January 1931 meeting in Freiburg with Finke, Heidegger and Martin Honecker. Heidegger appeared open, and Husserl seemed receptive when informed. Honecker, the chair of Roman Catholic philosophy, agreed to accept the proposal. By March 1931, Stein had resigned her position in Speyer and returned to Breslau to focus on her treatise, Potency and Act, and her lectures. At this time, Josef Koch approached her to teach a class on phenomenology at the University of Breslau, but the arrangement fell through.
By October, she had sent copies of the manuscript to Honecker, Heidegger, and Husserl. In the following month, the committee, acknowledged that her scholarship showed she was ready for a lectureship, but was told not to formally apply because her application could not be accepted. Ultimately, her inability to secure a position in Breslau and Freiburg stemmed from economic hardship and anti-Jewish bias, as well as departmental politics.


=== Münster, 1932–1933 ===

At the end of 1931, Stein was invited to be a lecturer at Münster's German Institute of Scientific Pedagogy, a Catholic teachers' college.  She had been recruited by founder Maria Schmitz, chair of the Association of Catholic German Women Teachers, who was impressed by Stein's Salzburg lecture, The Ethos of Women’s Professions. Stein began teaching in spring 1932. 
Stein continued pursuing an academic position, asking Conrad-Martius to critique her Habilitationsschrift, Potency and Act, while Honecker encouraged her to make contact with Catholic philosophers at the University of Münster.  In September 1932, the Société Thomiste invited her to a conference on Phenomenology and Thomism in Juvisy.  Designated the "Husserl expert", she was the sole woman at the conference and one of only five German attendees. She befriended philosopher Jacques Maritain and his wife, Raïssa, later attending one of their retreats in Meudon.
When the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service banned non-Aryans from public service on April 7, 1933, the institute's administration asked her to step down until the political issues were resolved, even though she wasn't a civil servant. That day, Stein visited Walzer to discuss traveling to Rome to petition Pope Pius XI for an encyclical. Walzer advised her to write a letter to the Pope urging him to condemn anti-Jewish actions, which Walzer forwarded with a supportive cover letter. The letter was received on April 20,  but the Pope remained silent. Stein preemptively resigned on April 20, 1933, though the Association of Catholic German Women Teachers continued to pay her salary. That same month, her mentor Husserl was dismissed from Freiburg.


== Carmelite vocation and martyrdom ==

Although Edith Stein spent eight years with the Dominicans in Speyer as a teacher and considered the Benedictine Abbey of Beuron, according to her own words, "like the antechamber of heaven", she never thought to become a Dominican or a Benedictine: "It always seemed to me as if the Lord had reserved something for me in Carmel that I could only find there". Stein entered the Discalced Carmelite Monastery St. Maria vom Frieden ("Our Lady of Peace") in Cologne-Lindenthal in October 1933 and took the religious name Teresia Benedicta a Cruce ("Teresa Benedicta of the Cross"). On her entry, she stated: "It is not human activity that can help us, but the suffering of Christ. To share in that is my desire."
On 21 April 1938, Edith Stein took her solemn vows. After her solemn vows, she was allowed to continue her philosophical writings. In Cologne she wrote her metaphysical book Endliches und ewiges Sein (Finite and Eternal Being), which attempted to combine the philosophies of Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus and Husserl. 

In December 1938, the order transferred Teresa Benedicta and her sister Rosa to the Discalced Carmelite monastery in Echt, near Roermond. This was done to avoid the growing Nazi threat. An earlier attempt to place and her sister with the Carmelite Daughters of the Divine Heart of Jesus in Le Pâquier, Switzerland, had failed due to the lack of necessary documents. In Echt, Teresa Benedicta wrote Studie über Joannes a Cruce: Kreuzeswissenschaft ("Studies on John of the Cross: The Science of the Cross"). In her testament of 9 June 1939 she wrote: I beg the Lord to take my life and my death … for all concerns of the Sacred Hearts of Jesus and Mary and the Holy Church, especially for the preservation, sanctification, and perfection of our holy order, in particular the Carmelite convents of Cologne and Echt, as atonement for the unbelief of the Jewish people, and that the Lord will be received by his own people and his kingdom shall come in glory, for the salvation of Germany and the peace of the world, at last for my loved ones, living or dead, and for all God gave to me: that none of them shall go astray.
Stein's move to Echt prompted her to be more devout and even more observant of the Carmelite rule. After having her teaching position revoked by the implementation of the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service, Stein quickly eased back into the role of instructor at the Monastery in Echt, teaching both fellow sisters and students within the community Latin and philosophy.
Even prior to the Nazi occupation of the Netherlands, Stein believed that she would not survive the war, going so far as to write to the prioress to request her permission to "allow [Stein] to offer [her]self to the heart of Jesus as a sacrifice of atonement for true peace" and she also wrote a will. Her fellow sisters would later recount how Stein began "quietly training herself for life in a concentration camp, by enduring cold and hunger" after the Nazi invasion of the Netherlands in May 1940.

Ultimately, she would not be safe in the Netherlands. The Dutch Bishops' Conference had a pastoral letter in which they protested against the persecution of the Jews; it was read in all churches across the nation on 20 July 1942. Subsequently, the Nazis declared the Catholics of Jewish origin to be the most dangerous enemies, who had to be deported immediately. In a retaliatory response on 26 July 1942 the Reichskommissar of the Netherlands, Arthur Seyss-Inquart, ordered the arrest of all converts who had previously been spared. Along with two hundred and forty-three baptised former Jews who were living in the Netherlands, Teresa Benedicta a Cruce was arrested by the SS on 2 August 1942. She and her sister Rosa were imprisoned at the concentration camps of Amersfoort and Westerbork before they were deported to Auschwitz. A Dutch official at Westerbork was so impressed by her sense of faith and her calm disposition, so he offered her an escape plan. Stein vehemently refused his assistance, stating: "If somebody intervened at this point and took away [her] chance to share in the fate of [her] brothers and sisters, that would be utter annihilation."
On 7 August 1942, early in the morning, 987 Jews were deported to the Auschwitz concentration camp. It was probably on 9 August that Teresa Benedicta of the Cross, her sister Rosa, and many more Jewish people were killed in a gas chamber at Birkenau.


== Philosophy ==
Stein's development as a philosopher is frequently divided into three periods: an early, phenomenological (1916–1925), a middle, comparative (1925–1933) and a late, Christian (1935–1942). In reality the same factors work themselves out throughout her work and propels it forward: 1. a profound understanding of and commitment to the phenomenological method as taught by Husserl and Reinach; 2. a deep sense of responsibility to the other for what we believe and 3. an acceptance of my own inability to form a complete, meaningful worldview without divine assistance. The three periods are best understood as stages of integration of these three factors, with Stein's baptism New Year's Day 1922, marking a decisive step on the way and her entering Carmel 14 October 1933 marking another.


=== The early phenomenological period (1916–1925) ===
Stein's dissertation on empathy was according to her own account an attempt to fill a gap in Husserl's work. In her autobiographical Life in a Jewish Family, she recalled that he took empathy to be the crucial act in which intersubjectivity was established, but nowhere detailed exactly what was meant by it. She therefore wanted to undertake this task and thereby clarify this crucial idea for the development of the phenomenological movement. While working as Husserl's assistant (1916–18) she edited Husserl's manuscripts of what was later to be published as Ideas II and III, and in the process came to understand the extraordinary importance this act has for our constitution of the intersubjective world, and in particular for the objects studied by psychology and the humanities. When she resigned from her position as Husserl's assistant, the first work she undertook was on the phenomenological constitution of those objects: the psyche and the spirit. The result was the two treatises of Philosophy of Psychology and the Humanities, published in Husserl's Jahrbuch 1922: Psychic Causality and Individual and Community. From this period also dates Introduction to Philosophy, An Investigation Concerning the State, and very importantly Freedom and Grace.


=== The middle comparative period (1925–1933) ===

Encouraged to study and compare Thomas Aquinas' philosophy with that of the phenomenological movement, Stein embarked on a translation project of Aquinas' De Veritate, which was to be published in two volumes in 1932. The work, which translates Aquinas' way of thinking into a modern German idiom and restyles it as a contemporary academic treatise, occasioned that Stein engaged with Aquinas' thought as a phenomenologist, i.e. as someone interested in the matters discussed by Aquinas, as distinct from providing an interpretation of Aquinas' thought or writing in prolongation of it as a thomist. The most important works from this period are 'Husserl and Aquinas: A Comparison', in which she discusses the differing methodologies of Husserl and Aquinas and accounts for their differences, Potency and Act, in which she attempts a phenomenological investigation of 'potency' and 'act' and the twin work of anthropology: The Structure of the Human Person. Philosophical Anthropology and What is the Human Being? Theological Anthropology (the second volume remains a highly developed draft rather than a completed work, since Stein's lectures were canceled in 1933). During this period she also lectures on women's education and vocation and on education in general to very large audiences and to great acclaim. In these lectures, published in ESGA 13 and ESGA 16, she works out for herself the important questions concerning social type and essence, which find a fuller development in The Structure of the Human Person.


=== The later Christian period (1934–1942) ===
Her first literal assignment in Carmel was to prepare Potency and Act for publication, a task she accomplished by writing a new book: Finite and Eternal Being – An Ascent to the Meaning of Being. This work proposed a phenomenological doctrine of being (Seinslehre), which knows itself to be Christian, i.e. as taking Christian Revelation to contribute towards the view of the world in which it looks for and finds the meaning of being in being's unfolding. Stein also worked on Dionysius the Areopagite, translating his works into German and writing (for him) a work supposed to be lost on symbolic theology. Stein's final work, the Science of the Cross, was a commentary on John of the Cross, which developed the specifically Carmelite understanding of the depths of the soul, already of interest to Stein in her early work.


== Veneration and legacy ==


=== Canonization ===
 
Immediately after World War II, nuns of the Carmel Monastery in Cologne began gathering documentation about Stein, including her letters. Posselt, now prioress, wrote a popular biography about her in 1947. Recognizing Stein's importance, Cardinal Joseph Frings, Archbishop of Cologne, sought special permission to open her canonization process in Cologne, as church protocol normally required causes to begin where the person died.
Permission was granted in 1958. The process began in 1962, aiming to establish Stein's heroic virtue. 
In 1980, Jakob Schlafke, Canon of Cologne Cathedral and vice postulator, published Nazi documents showing that hatred of the faith led to Stein's death, establishing her martyrdom. Cardinals Joseph Höffner of Cologne and Józef Glemp of Poland petitioned Pope John Paul II in 1983 to proceed with her cause as a martyr. Following simplified Vatican procedures introduced that same year, a January 26, 1987 decree promulgated her heroic virtue and martyrdom, confirming her as venerable. 
On May 1, 1987, Pope John Paul II issued an apostolic letter in Cologne confirming her beatification. Canonization required Vatican recognition of a single miracle. In May 1997, the pope formally recognized a miracle attributed to Stein's intercession, involving a three-year-old girl who recovered from a fatal dose of Tylenol after her parents prayed to Stein. The canonization decree was published in February 1998, and the ceremony took place in St. Peter's Square that October.


==== Controversy surrounding canonization ====
Stein's canonization prompted debate, with scholars and Jewish critics questioning her designation as a Christian martyr, maintaining that it appropriates a Jewish tragedy, obscures historic Catholic antisemitism, and implies supersessionism, reframing the genocide as redemptive suffering. Critics argue that Stein did not die in defense of Catholic doctrine but as one Jewish victim among the millions murdered under Nazi ideology. Others note that she voluntarily converted to a faith whose history of antisemitism helped enable the Shoah; as an apostate, she cannot represent Jewish victims or turn a Jewish tragedy into Christian martyrdom.
Stein's own theology of expiatory suffering, offering herself as atonement for what she called Jewish unbelief, further carries supersessionist overtones and conflicts with the Jewish rejection of vicarious atonement.
From the Catholic perspective, honoring Stein as a martyr and saint bridges Jewish and Christian heritage while ensuring the atrocities of the Shoah are never forgotten and fostering vigilance against future persecution. Proponents also emphasize that honoring Stein expresses solidarity with Jewish victims and confronts antisemitism. Defenders of her canonization present Stein as a unifying figure who retained pride in her Jewish heritage and refused to be spared from the suffering of her people. As both a daughter of Israel and a Catholic saint, her elevation is seen as sanctifying Jewish martyrdom and affirming the ongoing validity of God's covenant with the Jewish people.
Beyond institutional debates, other scholars emphasize Stein's own efforts to define her identity rather than being reduced to either side's narrative. Although her conversion led some to exclude her as a German-Jewish figure, she resisted rigid labels, maintaining a continuity of self that honored her Jewish heritage alongside her Catholic commitments. 
Viewing her inner spiritual life as an empathetic bridge to others and to God, she cultivated a personal synthesis that resists strict categorization as solely Jewish or Catholic.


=== Patron Saint of Europe ===
On October 1, 1999, Pope John Paul II proclaimed Teresa Benedicta of the Cross, along with Catherine of Siena and Bridget of Sweden, patrons of Europe, joining the patrons Benedict of Nursia and Cyril and Methodius. Selecting these women underscored women's role in Europe, recognizing their dignity and gifts in the Church and society, while balancing the number of male and female patrons to reflect their contributions to European society. The pope specifically cited Stein's advocacy for women's social status and exploration of their secular and religious missions. He emphasized that Stein's violent death alongside her Jewish people expresses solidarity with human suffering as an innocent victim and serves as a protest against every violation of the fundamental rights of the person. Through this proclamation, he intended to foster mutual respect and unity across religious, ethnic, and cultural divides.


=== Doctor of the Church petition ===
In April 2024, Father Miguel Márquez Calle, superior general of the Discalced Carmelites, petitioned Pope Francis to declare Stein a Doctor of the Church (Doctor Ecclesiae Universalis), proposing the specific title doctor veritatis ("doctor of truth"). The petition followed documentation gathered by an international committee commissioned by the order in 2022 on the 100th anniversary of her baptism and the 80th anniversary of her martyrdom. If approved, she would join the 37 saints, 33 men and four women, who hold the title. On June 10, 2024, however, the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints announced that Pope Francis had provisionally declined to open the formal procedure to elevate her. The Carmelites intend to resubmit the case.
Stein's case could be considered because the Dicastery for the Causes of Saints concluded in the 2010s that martyrdom, historically a procedural bar to becoming a Doctor of the Church, can attest to the truth of a saint's doctrine.  Instead, the central question is whether her writings demonstrate "eminent doctrine" (eminens doctrina): work that deepens theology, develops Christian thought, and exerts a lasting influence on the Church's spiritual life. Commentators have questioned whether Stein's writings offer original insights, arguing that she primarily synthesized the thoughts of others or noting that her biblical exegesis is weaker than her philosophy. Scholars also observe that many Doctors waited centuries to be named, and that too little time has passed since Stein's death to show the lasting impact of her teachings.   
Proponents contend that Stein's writings form a coherent whole directed toward the mystery of God (reductio ad mysterium), uniting rational inquiry with personal sacrifice. They suggest that she bridges the Old and New Testaments, integrates phenomenology with Thomistic metaphysics and the "science of the cross" (scientia Crucis), and develops a concrete anthropology exploring the spiritual dimension of the body, gender complementarity, and meaning in history. In his 1998 encyclical Fides et Ratio, Pope John Paul II, whose theological approach aligned with Stein's, included her as the only woman among the Christian thinkers who united philosophy and theology.
